Accelerating the Adoption of Residential Solar Power Systems: 

Policy Analysis using a Dynamic Structural Model 

Sebastián Souyris 1, Jason A. Duan 2, Anantaram Balakrishnan 2, Varun Rai 3

This version: June 30, 2026 

Problem definition: Solar electricity generation is a strategic component of energy portfolios to meet growing demand and reduce carbon emissions. To accelerate the installation of household solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, governmental agencies and municipalities offer upfront incentives such as rebates and tax credits. Designing effective incentive policies with limited budgets requires principled data-driven approaches that capture the drivers of residential PV adoption and the effects of incentives on the adoption rate. 

Methodology/results: We develop a dynamic structural model for the diffusion of residential PV systems by modeling the adoption decisions of forward-looking individual households who consider the economic trade-offs between installing a system now or later. This choice depends on both their return on investment and the influence by neighbors. Our model segments households by home values and urbanization level, incorporates unobserved heterogeneity, and captures the observed spatio-temporal dynamics of installations. We fit the model to a detailed dataset consisting of household-level information in Austin, Texas, and use Bayesian estimation to overcome the computational burden. In out-of-sample tests, our model predicts PV installations more accurately than other contemporary models. We simulate various counterfactual scenarios, with embedded dynamic equilibrium of PV diffusion, to generate insights into the design of effective rebate policies. The framework can also be adapted to study the adoption dynamics of other durable technologies. 

Managerial implications: Our counterfactual analysis shows that a rebate program offered over a limited duration is more effective than a prolonged and costly program to incent PV adoption and reduce carbon emissions. This counterintuitive result stems from households' forward-looking behavior, influence of neighboring adopters, and the consequent acceleration in the adoptions before the rebate ends. We also explore policies which reduce rebates in multiple steps and offer differentiated rebates for different segments. We find that a two-step rebate reduction is superior to multiple small reductions and geographically differentiated policies can improve policy performance, whereas differentiation based on home value yields little improvement over a uniform rebate policy. 

Keywords : solar photovoltaic, technology diffusion, dynamic discrete choice model, Bayesian structural estimation, counterfactual policy analysis, energy policy

1. Introduction 

With the growing demand for electricity, uncertainties in the cost of conventional fuels, and concerns about environmental impact, solar energy has emerged as an important component of energy portfolios for governments and society. By the end of 2025, cumulative U.S. solar capacity reached 279 GW with over five million installations, 97% of them residential rooftops on 7% of U.S. homes (SEIA and Wood Mackenzie 2026). Globally, installed solar capacity reached roughly 2.4 TW, with a record 511 GW added in 2025 alone (IRENA 2026). The cost of solar panels and associated hardware has decreased dramatically, from $10 per watt in 2007 to a benchmark market price of about $3.15 per watt by 2024 (Ramasamy et al. 2025), making the technology accessible and economically viable for many households. To accelerate solar photovoltaic (PV) adoption, governmental agencies and municipalities have provided upfront incentives such as equipment and installation rebates as well as tax credits that are proportional to the installed wattage. The design of such programs has become particularly important and relevant for state agencies and local utilities since their rebates are the principal policy levers to promote residential PV adoption after federal residential clean energy credit programs ended (SEIA and Wood Mackenzie 2026). Figure 1 shows the median cost of residential PV systems and the upfront incentives, both in dollars per watt, in the U.S. from 2000 to 2024. Since costs and incentives decreased at nearly the same rate during this period, the net installation cost remained relatively flat. Despite the stable net cost, the number of new PV installations, shown in Figure 1, follows a pattern similar to the diffusion of other new technologies. The adoption rate is relatively low during the “introduction” phase but then increases dramatically (around 2010) in the “growth” phase. Higher rebates can attract more adopters, but budgetary constraints limit both the magnitude and duration of rebate programs. Policy makers are interested in identifying effective incentive policies to maximize PV adoption and carbon savings using limited budgets. This paper seeks to address such policy-support needs in this and other similar contexts that entail deciding incentives to promote the adoption of expensive durable products. 

Assessing the impact of PV adoption incentives requires developing a data-driven model that captures the main factors that drive household adoption decisions, namely, the long-term economic benefits of

installing a PV system and the influence of neighboring adopters, to predict how adoption evolves as a function of the incentive policy. Both the economic and peer influence factors vary by household and with time. Installing a PV system requires a significant initial investment that may be recouped over the lifetime of the system through energy credits or savings in other energy costs. The net present value of the investment, including rebates, depends on the system size, time-varying cost, and other attributes specific to each property. Peer effects on PV adoption has been noted by researchers (e.g., Bollinger and Gillingham 2012) and supported by observations such as the following in the trade press: “people’s decision to go solar affects other people’s decision to go solar”, producing “a kind of viral activity” in adoptions (Woody 2022). In the PV installation context, peer influence stems from the visibility of rooftop panels on nearby properties installed by neighbors who have adopted the technology. It is household-specific, depending on the property’s location, and evolves over time as neighbors adopt. Finally, the adoption dynamics also need to account for the finite duration of rebate programs. For instance, industry analysts note that PV installers may experience a “run on the bank,” a surge in demand, before the incentives expire. 

To support policy decisions on upfront PV installation incentives, we first develop a dynamic structural model for each household’s adoption decision based on a detailed dataset on attributes (such as demographics, property characteristics and year it was built, and electricity consumption) of individual residences of actual or potential PV adopters in and around Austin, Texas. The utility function in this dynamic discrete choice model (DDCM) is time-varying and household-dependent. It consists of two main components: an economic component represented by the net present value (NPV) of installing a PV system (investment cost less upfront incentives and savings/credits for future PV electricity generation), and a neighborhood effect that is proportional to the number of neighbors within a specified radial distance who have already installed PV systems. In each period (quarter), households that have not yet installed a PV system are forward-looking, i.e., they decide whether or not to adopt in this period by comparing the utility of adopting now versus deferring adoption to a future period. This dynamic comparison entails considering not only the costs and benefits of later adoption but also estimating the endogenous number of future neighboring adopters. By aggregating the likelihood of adoption over all households in every period, we can estimate the total adoption rate over time. 

This dynamic structural model, based on individual household decisions, provides two main advantages. First, since our model begins with estimates of primitive parameters in the consumer utility functions, it permits counterfactual analysis of alternative incentive policies that differ in the amount and duration of the incentive offered. In contrast, in reduced-form models, the parameters estimated from historical data are generally not invariant with respect to changes in policies (e.g., those outside the observed ranges of rebates and program durations), as highlighted by the Lucas critique (Lucas 1976, Nevo and Whinston 2010). Second, by treating each household as the unit of analysis, we can accommodate household-specific utility values based on house size, system capacity, costs and future 3

savings, actual neighborhood composition, wealth and geographic segment, and unobserved heterogeneity in attitudes towards renewable energy. Models based on more aggregate data, e.g., by zip code or region, cannot incorporate these refinements. As we elaborate in Section 2, existing empirical models of PV adoption have different objectives: most are reduced-form models that are not suited for counterfactual analysis of upfront incentive policies, and the few available structural models do not consider peer effects or household-level heterogeneity. We are not aware of any previous empirical study that provides policy insights on the level and duration of upfront PV incentives and the trade-offs among long-run adoption, carbon emissions, and program budgets. 

For our empirical analysis, we first combine the data on demographics, property characteristics, period built, and electricity consumption of each household with information about the attributes of the PV system needed for each property, installation costs, and the timing of PV system installation by households that have already adopted. To estimate the structural parameters that govern adoption behavior, we design and implement a Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo method that allows us to handle unobserved heterogeneity in a dynamic programming model. Within our structural modeling framework, we calibrate alternative model specifications (e.g., by varying the radial distance that defines the influencing neighborhood of each household, with and without household-level heterogeneity, and so on) and select the model with the lowest out-of-sample prediction error. Our estimation results indicate that peer influence from neighbors within a mile radius is most salient, and wealthier households are more inclined to install PV systems earlier, consistent with the large upfront investment a system requires. To assess the effectiveness of our modeling approach, we compare the predictive performance of our model with that of alternative models, including the myopic (not forward-looking) variant of our model, a hazard model recently applied by Bollinger et al. (2022) on disaggregate household-level data, and a Bass model to forecast aggregate adoption trends. Our DDCM with forward-looking households and neighborhood effects outperforms these models in out-of-sample prediction error. 

We next exercise our model to identify effective upfront incentive policies for accelerating PV diffusion with a limited budget. We begin with a class of policies that has two levers, the initial net cost (net of rebates) and the period in which the rebate is terminated or equivalently the rebate duration. We conduct a counterfactual analysis by varying both, and evaluating the effectiveness of each policy on three dimensions: (a) PV penetration, i.e., the cumulative number of adopters and PV megawatts installed; (b) the total funds or budget spent on the rebate program; and (c) the total carbon savings due to PV installations. Our analysis generates an efficient frontier of policies from which policymakers can select the one that best meets their relative preferences for the three dimensions. Our simulation reveals that adoptions accelerate toward the end of a rebate program as households anticipate the rebate’s expiration. Consequently, offering rebates for a longer duration (which is more costly) does not necessarily increase adoption or savings in carbon emissions. Two features of our model contribute to this counterintuitive result – forward-looking adoption decisions and the peer influence of neighboring adopters. Offering a 4

higher initial rebate increases PV adoption and carbon savings but requires more budget spending; under budget constraints, programs with moderate rebates and durations can outperform policies that offer smaller subsidies for longer durations or higher subsidies for shorter durations. We also study the impact of gradually reducing the rebate over multiple rebate switching periods, and offering differentiated rebate policies where the rebates vary by geographic or economic segments. 

This paper focuses on residential PV adoption, using data from the Austin metropolitan area since the incentives are decided and offered by local agencies. Our model and estimation methodology readily applies to PV adoption in other geographical areas that may have different household characteristics, demographics, and attitudes towards PV technology. More broadly, the structural framework can be adapted to study durable technologies characterized by substantial upfront investment, policy-dependent incentives, peer influence, and forward-looking adoption timing. Although prior research on durable goods has recognized the importance of these features, to our knowledge, none has incorporated all four in a single empirical model. In Section 7, we elaborate on these broader application contexts and discuss how the framework can be extended to technologies such as electric vehicles and heat pumps through modest modifications to the model specification, while using the same modeling and estimation approach. 

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the related literature. Section 3 discusses the data used to develop and calibrate our model. Section 4 develops the dynamic structural model and the estimation algorithm. Section 5 presents and interprets the estimation results. Section 6 discusses our counterfactual analysis and policy insights, and Section 7 concludes the paper. All the Appendices are provided in the e-companion. 

2. Literature Review 

Following the sustainability research roadmap in operations management proposed by Atasu et al. (2019), this paper, as a policy-oriented study of the diffusion of PV technology, lies in the intersection of two major streams of the sustainability literature: the low-carbon economy and technological innovation. It addresses the key challenge of evaluating counterfactual incentive policies to promote solar panel adoption. For this purpose, we employ a dynamic structural modeling approach, adding to the stream of operations literature to address policy questions in other operations contexts (e.g., Olivares et al. 2008, Musalem et al. 2010, Li et al. 2014, Bray and Mendelson 2015, Wang et al. 2022, Bravo et al. 2025). 

2.1. Empirical Studies of PV Diffusion 

Traditionally, empirical modeling of product and technology diffusion uses the Bass model framework to forecast market penetration at the aggregate level. These approaches have been extended and applied to various contexts, incorporating factors such as pricing, marketing mix, technological innovations, consumer income levels, and network connections (e.g., Van den Bulte and Stremersch 2004, Goldenberg et al. 2010, Shaikh et al. 2010). As reduced-form models, they are not amenable to counterfactual analysis of the drivers of diffusion outside the observed ranges. 

Focusing on PV adoption, a growing body of empirical papers uses household and regional-level data 5

to analyze various aspects of the diffusion of household PV systems. Table 1 positions our paper relative to the most closely related studies along five key modeling features. 

Klein and Deissenroth (2017) study residential PV adoption in Germany under feed-in tariffs (FIT), i.e., credits for PV electricity generation, using a model based on prospect theory and loss aversion. Candas et al. (2019) develop a model of PV adoption in Germany and Italy to examine how profitability, public opinion, and self-consumption affect PV diffusion and the potential phase-out of FIT. To compare the effectiveness of upfront incentives versus subsidies for future electricity production, De Groote and Verboven (2019) propose a dynamic structural model with forward-looking consumers, but without peer effects and with heterogeneity only at the regional level. Their counterfactual analysis suggests that upfront incentives through subsidies can achieve greater adoption of PV technology at a much lower budgetary cost. Gillingham and Bollinger (2021) conduct a randomized experiment to compare the impact of a group pricing scheme on the adoption rate for installing PV systems. Bollinger et al. (2022) investigate the components and reasons underlying peer effects from neighbors' solar installations using an econometric model based on the single-period utility of installing PV systems. Finally, Langer and Lemoine (2022) develop a dynamic structural model to investigate household intertemporal preferences and the design of subsidies over time.

2.2. Analytical Models of PV Diffusion and Incentives 

A stream of analytical papers employs stylized models to investigate how government subsidies influence PV adoption and how tariff structures and compensation schemes for PV electricity impact social welfare. Cohen et al. (2016) assess the effects of government incentives on consumer adoption, considering demand uncertainty when designing subsidies to achieve PV adoption targets. Sunar and Swaminathan (2021) analyze how solar panel deployment and net-metering policy affect the electric utility business, and find that utilities can benefit from strategies that motivate their customers to adopt PV. Using a sequential game model, Agrawal et al. (2022) show that a higher generation subsidy increases total adoption whereas a higher investment subsidy can lower it, so third-party ownership (leasing) can be more effective. Singh and Scheller-Wolf (2021) incorporate competition in the solar marketplace and examine how various tariff structures align with social objectives. Yu et al. (2018) investigate structural conditions under which it is more efficient for a government to subsidize consumers or manufacturers to maximize social welfare. To understand the relationship between incentives and solar equipment supply, Chemama et al. (2019) study flexible subsidy policies when the government faces a strategic PV supplier. Alizamir et al. (2016), Babich et al. (2020), and others analyze the impact of different FIT structures to design more effective programs to incentivize PV adoption. More recently, Mamaghani and Çakanyildirim (2023) focus on pricing mechanisms to mitigate the power utility death-spiral caused by the adoption of distributed solar power generation. Kaps et al. (2023) analyze the synergy between solar power and storage for off-grid electrification. 

3. Residential PV Systems Data 

Our study is based on information from Austin, Texas, which is served by a single electric utility, Austin Energy, a publicly owned entity that provides electricity for most of the city and some suburbs. For consumers who install PV systems, the local electric utility reduces the household’s electricity bill by awarding credits for the electricity generated by the PV system. For every kWh the system produces, the 7

utility credits the household at a residential solar energy rate, which Austin Energy calls the Value-of-Solar rate (a type of FIT). If the total solar credits for a month exceed the electricity consumed, the excess credits roll over to the next month. In practice, a credit rollover seldom occurs because the installed PV systems are often sized to produce less electricity per month than the household consumes. In addition to electricity bill credits, households that install PV systems also received rebates and tax credits from federal and local authorities during the period of our study. 

Austin Energy’s service area contains about 170,000 single-family residential households. Our dataset covers all adopters and non-adopters from 2004 to 2013. During that period, the housing stock growth was approximately 2% per year. In total, 2,724 households adopted and installed PV systems. To identify potential PV adopters among the remaining households, we employ Rai and Robinson’s (2015) approach based on the value of a control variable called perceived behavioral control (PBC), a composite metric of home market value, house size, tree cover, and irradiance received. Installation is not economical for households with low irradiance or high tree cover (see Rai and Robinson, 2015, for details); since such households have a non-positive PBC value, we consider only households with a positive PBC value as potential adopters. For our dataset, this approach identified 42,384 households as potential adopters (including the actual adopters up to 2013). We obtained the characteristics of individual households (property values, land parcel information) from the Travis County Appraisal District’s (TCAD) records (www.traviscad.org) for all appraised properties in the county. For each residential property in the Austin Energy service area, we extracted the following data from the TCAD database: property coordinates and land area; total constructed building area; period built; appraised market value (in 2012); and whether a PV system can be installed on the property. We exclude commercial buildings, apartment buildings, and other ineligible properties. Further, for each household that has already installed a PV system, we obtained from Austin Energy the following information about their systems: application date for solar rebate program; system installation date; total PV system cost (including PV panel and hardware price, and installation cost) quoted at the time of application; system size in watts; rebate in $ per watt; and, estimated annual electricity savings in kWh. The data reveal that the average lead time between the date a household applies for the rebate program and the installation date is roughly one quarter (139 days). 4. Dynamic Discrete Choice Model 

Based on a long research survey of PV adoption in Texas, Rai and McAndrews (2012) found that the following factors are important when a household decides whether to adopt and install a PV system: 

(i) The future cash flow from generating electricity using a PV system. PV systems require a large upfront investment, so the multi-year savings in electricity charges over the system’s lifetime are an important economic determinant. 

(ii) Consideration of inter-temporal trade-offs between adopting a PV system today versus deferring adoption. Adopting now captures available government incentives and starts the electricity savings immediately, whereas deferring may lower the system cost but risks missing the incentives. 

(iii) The predisposition of a potential adopter to install a PV system. This predisposition increases as more neighboring households install PV systems: visible installations signal that the technology is reliable and cost-effective and also serve as advertising. These neighborhood influences increase the likelihood of adoption. 

Models of PV adoption should therefore incorporate inter-temporal economic trade-offs and the effects of the installed base in the decision maker’s neighborhood; we next propose a dynamic discrete choice model with these features.

4.1.3. State variables, transition equations, and other model specifications

We treat 𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 as an exogenous state variable since the net cost of installation is determined by supply and demand in the broader economy rather than the actions of individual households in a particular locality. The institutional record supports this treatment: Austin Energy sets the rebate through an annual budgeting process that accounts for changes in PV panel and installation prices, and once the city council approves the budget, the posted dollar-per-watt rebate applies uniformly to all qualifying applicants for that year. The rebate is therefore neither responsive to individual adoption decisions nor negotiated for individual households. The remaining components of the net cost, the federal ITC and the solar energy rate, were likewise fixed by statute or through citywide proceedings, outside individual household transactions.

4.1.4. Model identification 

We conclude this section on model development by addressing the identification of model parameters. We fix the discount factor, which is generally unidentified in dynamic discrete choice models (Magnac and Thesmar 2002). The state transition densities are identified directly from the observed cost and installation histories. Given these transition densities, the conditional choice probabilities identify the utility parameters through the Hotz and Miller (1993) inversion and the nested fixed-point structure of Rust (1987). The coefficient of NPV 𝑖𝑖 𝑡𝑡 (𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 ) is identified from the variation in net cost over households and time, induced by the cost and rebate path and interacted with household-specific system size. The neighborhood effect is identified from the variation in neighbor-adopter counts within specific radial rings for each household over time. The baseline utility is pinned down by the aggregate adoption level, while the heterogeneity variance is identified from residual variation in utility across households. Appendix A.1 provides the mathematical treatment of the identification conditions. 

One remaining concern is that the annual rebate budget may have been set in anticipation of aggregate demand shocks in the following year. Such policy endogeneity could confound the estimated effect of NPV 𝑖𝑖 𝑡𝑡 (𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 ). To assess whether the rebate could be endogenous and bias our estimates, we conduct an empirical test of whether household NPV 𝑖𝑖 𝑡𝑡 (𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 ) has different effects on PV adoption depending on whether we include period-specific temporal fixed effects that control for unobserved demand shocks over time. In our model, NPV 𝑖𝑖 𝑡𝑡 (𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 ) is the only variable that depends on the rebate. We find that the estimated effect of NPV 𝑖𝑖 𝑡𝑡 (𝑐𝑐𝑡𝑡 ) remains essentially unchanged when temporal fixed effects are included, alleviating this endogeneity concern. We report the empirical test details and provide additional institutional information about the rebate in Appendix A.2.

Due to the large number of households, evaluating each household's value and likelihood functions is computationally burdensome. We therefore aggregate non-adopting households into clusters with similar characteristics (home market value, house size, tree cover, received irradiance) within each census block group (www2.census.gov). Our cluster-based likelihood adapts Manski and Lerman’s (1977) weighted choice-based sampling method to clusters. In Appendix B, we provide details of the clustering method and the corresponding weighted log-likelihood function. We prove in Proposition EC.1 that, asymptotically, this weighted cluster-based log-likelihood yields the same MLE as the log-likelihood function based on individual household data. The posterior mean from the Bayesian estimation also converges to the MLE asymptotically. This result applies to our analysis, since our clustering method yields a large number of clusters (over 1,100 clusters of size greater than one). 

We implemented the estimation and the counterfactual simulation procedures in Julia 1.12 (Bezanson et al. 2017). Our MCMC sampler reaches its stationary distribution within the first 2,500 iterations, which we discard as burn-in (Appendix D); we then run 18,000 iterations of the sampling chain for just under 4 hours on a single processor (Intel Xeon w3-2435, 3.1 GHz, 128 GB of memory), so that the posterior summaries are computed from a large post-convergence sample. Appendix D reports the sampler’s convergence diagnostics and the sensitivity of the estimates to longer runs. The workstation can run up to eight independent model estimations in parallel with adequate CPU and memory headroom, useful to concurrently estimate model variants and conduct robustness checks. For counterfactual simulations described in Section 6, a single policy path solves the rational expectations equilibrium in about five outer-loop iterations and roughly 80 minutes of elapsed time (Appendix C).

To achieve good model fit, we considered many alternative model specifications in equation  (1)  and compared their out-of-sample predictive performance. The different models vary in: (i) the economic segmentation 𝐸𝐸 of households by wealth, which we proxy by the properties’ appraised value; (ii) the sizes of the radial rings for models with neighborhood effects; and, (iii) the presence or omission of unobserved heterogeneity modeled by the random effect ( 𝜉𝜉𝑖𝑖 ). First, the models with neighborhood effects (rows (2), (4), and (6)) outperform the models without them (rows (1), (3), and (5)). Second, Appendix E extends the comparison to additional specifications: a larger radial ring, or rings beyond one mile, adds only a negligible neighborhood effect. The out-of-sample prediction errors of the leading models using a one-mile radial ring versus a half-mile radial ring differ by less than 2 adopters. 6. Counterfactual Policy Analysis 

Because the estimated structural parameters are invariant to policy changes, our model can evaluate the impact of alternative incentive policies on metrics of interest. For our counterfactual analysis, we focus on adjusting the upfront rebate 𝑟𝑟𝑡𝑡 ($ per watt installed) that a local government provides to households for installing a PV system and the duration 𝜏𝜏 of the rebate program with the goal of improving the program’s effectiveness in stimulating PV adoption and reducing carbon emissions, given a limited budget. 

Because the rebate budget is limited, rebates are offered only for a limited period. In Austin, the local utility announces its rebate schedule (the program duration and the amount of rebate offered over time) to residential customers well in advance; rebates are set higher initially and decline over time. The declining schedule induces households to advance their installations ahead of the scheduled reductions, a strategic-timing response documented in practice (Reeves and Rai 2018). We consider four key long-run metrics: (i) Cumulative Adoptions; (ii) Cumulative Megawatts (MW) of PV electricity generation installed; (iii) Cumulative Budget spent on rebates; and (iv) Carbon Emissions savings. We compute these metrics at period 56, which is less than six years beyond our estimation window (ending at quarter 33, 2012Q1). 20 

The counterfactual simulations initialize on the first 16 quarters of observed adoption and generate new adoption from period 17 onward. 

Our data show that, as PV technology costs decline, government rebates also tend to decrease correspondingly (Figures 1 and 2(a)), resulting in a net cost that remains relatively flat over time. Accordingly, to limit the number of policies to explore, we consider rebate schedules that generally maintain a constant net cost but are ‘switched,’ i.e., reduced in a few large steps, during the rebate duration. We organize the counterfactual analysis around the program designer’s problem: choosing a rebate schedule to maximize cumulative adoption, installed capacity, or carbon savings subject to a budget. Each subsection answers one design question: Section 6.1 determines when a program with a given initial net cost should terminate (a one-step rebate switching policy); Section 6.2 examines how the initial rebate level shifts the attainable outcomes; Section 6.3 assesses whether reducing the rebate in two or three steps outperforms a single switch; Section 6.4 evaluates rebate policies differentiated across household segments; the section closes with an out-of-sample validation and a robustness check of the simulation horizon’s ending period. Appendix C presents the simulation algorithm we developed for our counterfactual analysis; a key feature is the computation of the dynamic equilibrium across all households, where the state transition function for the number of neighboring adopters is determined by the equilibrium between the beliefs about future adoption and actual adoption across all households. 

6.1. One-Step Rebate Switching Policies 

In this first scenario, we set the rebate level so that the net cost starts at a “low” initial value of $1.34 per watt, which is the net cost in the early periods in our dataset, and rises to a “high” value of $2.49 per watt, the long-term PV cost (after it has stabilized), when the rebate program ends. The key decision variable is the timing of the “switch” from the low to the high net cost. We parametrize the alternative policies by the the Rebate Switching Period (RSP) and denote the one-step policy that switches at period τ as RSP(τ), for 

τ = 20, 24, …, 52, spanning early to late switching at 4 -period (1-year) intervals over the 56-period (quarterly) horizon. If the RSP exceeds period 38, when the PV cost has stabilized (see Figure 2(a)), the 

rebate reduces from $(2.49 − 1.34) = $1.15 per watt to zero in this period; if τ < 38, the rebate remains 

positive (since PV cost exceeds $2.49) until period 38, at which time it reduces to zero. 

The simulated adoption paths reveal the first feature a program designer must anticipate: households race a pre-announced deadline. Under each policy, cumulative adoption follows the typical diffusion-of-innovation curve, driven by initial lower net costs and peer influence, but the adoption rate rises as the pre-announced RSP approaches and drops once the rebate ends, producing a “kink” in the adoption curve at the switching period (Figure 4(a)). Appendix D presents the adoption trajectory under RSP(40), which reaches about 8,000 households by the end of the horizon, or about 19% of the total potential adopters. The acceleration just before rebate termination stems from the interplay between two features of our model: forward-looking households and neighborhood effects. From equation (1), the adoption utility in each period is increasing in the number of neighboring households that have installed PV systems. 21 

Without rebates, a household with a lower value of 𝜉𝜉ᵢ (household-dependent heterogeneity) will tend to wait until a sufficiently large number of neighbors have adopted. As the pre-announced termination date approaches, however, the benefit of the lower net cost outweighs the benefit of waiting for more neighboring adopters, and so the adoption curve starts out flatter but accelerates as the RSP approaches. 

Next, we compare the long-run metrics across the one-step RSP policies, including two extreme 

benchmarks: RSP(∞), which offers rebates indefinitely, and RSP(0), which offers no rebate. Figures 4(a) –4(d) show, respectively, the cumulative number of adopters, cumulative MW installed, cumulative budget spent on rebates, and total carbon savings over the time horizon for the different policies evaluated. As expected, RSP(0) has the lowest adoption curve among all the policies because it entails the highest net 

cost for households. But, surprisingly, the adoption curve of RSP(∞) is dominated by all the other policies with fixed rebate switching periods. Although RSP(∞) offers the lowest net cost over time, households 

are more likely to wait because the rebates never expire; as more households wait, the neighborhood effects weaken, further reducing adoption. Offering the rebate indefinitely therefore leads to lower adoption over time and is a dominated policy.

To verify that the policy implications are robust to the choice of simulation horizon, we conduct additional analyses in which we vary the end period (current period 56) used to measure installed capacity, carbon savings, and budget expenditures. Specifically, we reconstruct the cost-effectiveness frontier at five horizons, using periods 40 through 56 as the end of horizon, in four-period increments. The policy implications hold at every horizon: an intermediate switch yields the most carbon savings per budget dollar, installed capacity exhibits diminishing returns to budget, and deferring the switch past the interior optimum raises spending while lowering cumulative carbon. These findings demonstrate that the policy insights obtained from our model are not sensitive to the end period used to evaluate policy performance. Appendix I provides further details. 

6.2. Diffusion and Budgetary Implications of Varying the Initial Net Cost 

Extending the analyses of RSP(τ) policies, we next vary the initial net cost (while retaining one -step switching) to examine how the rebate level shifts the attainable outcomes and to identify the least-budget design for a given capacity or carbon-savings target. 

We conduct counterfactual policy simulations for the RSP(τ) policies using initial net costs that are 

20% lower ($1.072 per watt) and 20% higher ($1.608 per watt) than the baseline initial net cost of $1.34 per watt from the data. Figure 5 plots the cumulative MW installed and carbon savings versus budget for the lower (dotted blue) and higher (dashed green) initial net costs alongside the baseline (solid red). The relationships retain the same shape or pattern across the different initial net costs, confirming the 23 

robustness of our results to the initial rebate value. As we would expect, offering the highest rebate (lowest net cost) yields the maximum penetration and highest carbon savings, but requires large budgets. 

The frontier curves in Figure 5 let policymakers tune rebate policies to their objectives. For instance, suppose a policymaker has a fixed budget of, say $35 million, and wishes to maximize penetration. Then, offering an intermediate rebate level (initial net cost of $1.34) with an RSP of between 40 and 44 is most effective. Similarly, with a $35 million budget, carbon savings are maximized with moderate initial rebate and RSP around 36. That is, in the face of this budget constraint, a moderate incentive with moderate duration is better than offering a low initial rebate (high initial net cost of $1.608) for a longer duration or a high initial rebate (low initial net cost of $1.072 per watt) for a shorter duration. Of course, when budgets are highly restrictive, a low initial rebate becomes necessary. If policymakers aim to achieve a target cumulative MW installed, say 36 MW, then offering a lower rebate with a switching period of τ = 

40 achieves the goal with the lowest budget. However, a higher target, say 40 MW, is attainable only by providing a high initial rebate with RSP of 48. Interestingly, for all three initial net cost alternatives, RSP(36) or RSP(40) represents the inflection point in the plot of MW installed versus total budget and also achieves the highest carbon savings. In other words, these limited duration policies best ensure a high marginal return on the incremental rebate budget, regardless of the initial value of the net cost.

7. Conclusions 

Electricity generated by residential PV systems can lower households’ utility bills while reducing carbon emissions, yet adoption remains in the early phase of its diffusion curve, held back by high purchase and installation costs and by consumers’ uncertainty about the benefits. Effective incentive programs should therefore focus on reducing net costs and leveraging peer influence among consumers. 

To guide the design of such programs, we developed a dynamic structural model of individual households’ adoption decisions, estimated on a 9-year household-level dataset, that captures the economic benefits, neighborhood effects, forward-looking strategic behavior, and unobserved heterogeneity in households’ predisposition towards solar energy across economic and geographic segments. The estimates and out-of-sample prediction performance validate the importance of peer influence from nearby installations and the inclusion of forward-looking behavior in the model. Indeed, adding these factors reduces the out-of-sample adoption prediction error compared to benchmark models. Moreover, the structural model permits the evaluation of counterfactual policies. 

Our counterfactual simulations show that extending the rebate indefinitely is not optimal for either diffusion or budgets since forward-looking households postpone their adoptions, weakening neighborhood effects. For policymakers with both goals of increasing adoption and carbon savings with a limited rebate budget, the simulations characterize the trade-off curve from which agencies can select their preferred policy. 

This structural framework can be applied to other durable technologies that share four key features, namely that (1) adoption requires a large one-time investment with positive NPV due to ongoing benefits; (2) public policy incentives, such as rebates and credits, shape the effective price path; (3) adoption decisions are observable to nearby decision-makers, generating accumulated local peer exposure; and (4) decision-makers are forward-looking, timing their adoption in response to anticipated prices and incentives. These features are well-recognized in the literature on durable goods adoption, where strategic consumers time their purchases in response to market dynamics. For instance, appliance buyers retime purchases to capture rebates (Houde and Aldy 2017), and air-conditioner buyers trade off purchase prices against discounted future operating costs (Rapson 2014). The literature on hybrid and electric vehicles illustrates all four features. Hybrid-vehicle buyers time purchases around pre-announced tax-credit step-downs (Sallee 2011), and both the type of incentive offered, its size, terms of payment, and charging station density matter for adoption (Gallagher and Muehlegger 2011, Li et al. 2017, Springel 2021, Avci et al. 2015, Shi et al. 2022). Visibility matters as well, since car purchases increase purchase likelihood most where they are visually observable (McShane et al. 2012) and consumers pay a premium for hybrid models whose distinctive design visibly signals their environmental commitment (Sexton and Sexton 2014). Beyond vehicles, profitability-driven adoption with learning from neighbors is documented for agricultural technologies (Foster and Rosenzweig 1995, Conley and Udry 2010), and forward-looking benefits shape mobile telephony adoption (Björkegren 2019). To our knowledge, none of the literature 28 

above incorporates all four features in an empirical model. Our modeling framework can be adapted to provide policy support for incentive-driven adoption of durable goods with these features, such as including electric vehicles, home chargers, heat pumps, home emergency generators, and battery storage. Table 7 summarizes the simple modifications that two representative applications, electric vehicles and heat pumps, require; the estimation and counterfactual machinery remains unchanged. 

Future research may incorporate additional household information, such as prior investment in other green technologies or exposure to communication channels such as social media, to reduce the unobserved heterogeneity and improve model fit. Other fruitful directions include validating the PV adoption model on data from other cities, less densely populated areas, or developing countries; developing seeding strategies for faster diffusion; using our model as the basis for determining optimal incentive policies; and applying it to the diffusion of other capital-intensive technologies with peer influence.

A.2. Institutional Background and Exogeneity Evidence 

The net cost that drives adoption in our model is determined in part by the residential PV rebate, and the institutional record shows that Austin Energy set this rebate through an administrative budget process rather than in response to individual adoption decisions. Austin Energy funded the rebate from a fixed annual budget approved in the City of Austin budget cycle and disbursed it as a posted dollars-per-watt rate that applied uniformly to every qualifying applicant. That is, it did not negotiate the subsidy household by household or condition it on a neighborhood’s adoption history. The same administratively recorded schedule is described in prior publications (Rai and Robinson 2015, Rai and Sigrin 2013). When the installation-cost benchmarks used in the budgeting process changed, Austin Energy revised the posted rate administratively and announced the new rate publicly for the next fiscal year. The other components of the net cost include the federal investment tax credit by statute (U.S. Congress 2005, 2008) and the Value-of-Solar tariff, which replaced residential net metering on October 1, 2012, through a separate citywide proceeding (Austin Energy 2017, DSIRE 2013). The observed posted rebate rates in our data display a small number of discrete downward revisions over the time window. The program started in 2004 at $5.00 per watt and then held near $4.50 through fiscal years 2005–2008 (Solar Austin 2011). At the beginning of fiscal 2009 the rate fell to $3.75. It was then cut to $2.00, effective immediately, on June 11, 2012, and to about $1.50 in mid-2013. Two features of this process support treating the net cost as exogenous to the individual adoption decision. First, the rate changes were infrequent, discrete, and downward-only, and each was triggered by a documented decline in installation cost (a 17% decline in 2010 and an 11% decline over the first half of 2011), rather than by any household’s characteristics or by local adoption (Solar Austin 2011). Second, the rate was posted and applied uniformly, so an individual applicant could neither bargain over the subsidy nor reliably time it. One remaining concern is that Austin Energy may set the annual rebate budget and rate in anticipation of aggregate demand shocks in the following year. To further assess whether the rebate rate could be endogenous and bias our estimates, we conduct an empirical test of whether household net present value NPV it has different effects on PV adoption depending on whether we include period-specific temporal fixed effects that control for unobserved demand shocks over time (Wooldridge 2010, Chapter 10). In our model, NPV it is the only variable that is a function of the rebate rate. Because the dependent variable of installation is a quarterly dummy variable over time, we use the following reduced discrete hazard model 

G (λit ) = ξz + ηt + β1NPV it + β2Hit + β3Xi,

where the hazard rate λit of installation is modeled by the complementary log-log ( log[ − log[1 − λit ]] ) function. Here, 

ηt is the fixed effect that captures the unobserved aggregate temporal demand shocks for all households in the city, ξz is the zip-code fixed effect, Xi represents household characteristics, and Hit is the number of neighborhood installations within the one-mile radial ring. If the rebate rate is endogenous because of the anticipation of aggregate demand shocks, we should expect NPV it to be correlated with ηt. Therefore, the model that omits ηt,

G (λit ) = ξz + β1NPV it + β2Hit + β3Xi,

should produce a materially different estimate of the coefficient β1. Counterfactual Policy Analysis: Simulation Algorithm 

Simulations based on Bayesian estimation usually propagate every posterior draw through the simulation. However, we use the posterior means ¯θ = {¯ρ, ¯α, ¯γ, ¯ξ} as the estimates of the structural parameters to reduce the computational Online Supplement Author: Accelerating Residential Solar Adoption 11 burden because our policy simulation algorithm involves solving for an equilibrium that requires a large number of iterations, as we explain next. To simulate a specific policy scenario’s outcome, we compute the value function and the probabilities of adoption using the fitted value iteration algorithm (see, for example, Stachurski (2009)). Since our best Model (4)H includes only one radial ring, the state variable representing the number of neighboring adopters is a scalar hit . Note that, in the dynamic decision problem, hit is endogenous because it is the aggregation of the adoption decisions made by the neighbors of household i. Since hit is unobserved in the simulated counterfactual scenarios, we need to compute the dynamic equilibrium to obtain the state transition function for hit based on the household’s rational expectations. Our simulation algorithm uses an outer loop to compute this state transition equilibrium. We assume that, at each period t, household i uses the transition function hi,t +1 = λihit , with λi ≥ 1, the conditional mean of the transition in equation (C.8), to predict the cumulative number of its neighboring adopters in the next period until the total number of adopters equals the number of potential adopters ˆhi in its neighborhood; after reaching this capacity, hit remains constant. Observe that we permit the growth rate λi to vary by household. After simulating the new adopters in each period t, we re-calculate hit and re-estimate λi as the updated state transition function. We iterate this process until the number of adopters per period and λi converge; these iterations constitute the outer loop of our simulation algorithm. Because the PV system has a finite lifespan of 20 years (80 quarters), inside each outer loop we compute the finite-horizon value function using backward induction. For every household i and h = 1 , . . . , ˆhi, we first use linear interpolation and the fitted value iteration algorithm to calculate the value function in the last period t = T . Then, for any period t < T , we use backward induction to calculate the value function. Specifically, the recursive representation of the value function is

Stability of the structural parameters across specifications 

This section tests the robustness of our results to the model specification, extending the comparison in Table EC.2 across the economic segmentations E and the neighborhood radial rings D. Two facts anchor the comparison. First, extending the neighborhood beyond one mile adds only a negligible neighborhood effect: in the two-ring models (9), (12), and (15), the coefficient on adopters beyond one mile is near −4 × 10 −4, three orders of magnitude below the inner-ring coefficient (Table EC.4). Second, the leading one-mile and half-mile specifications predict almost equally well: their out-of-sample MAEs differ by less than 2% (for the two-segment models, 31.85 for Model (4)H against 31.37 for Model (10)H). Since predictive accuracy alone does not separate these specifications, we examine the struc-tural parameter estimates, reported for the twelve heterogeneity (H) specifications with neighborhood effects, models (2), (4), (6), and (7)–(15). The estimates are stable across these alternative specifications (Tables EC.3 and EC.4 and Figure EC.4). The het-erogeneity standard deviation σξ is statistically indistinguishable across the twelve specifications, its 95% credible intervals all overlapping near 0.051 . The baseline utility ρ stays within the narrow band [−6.85 , −6.64] and is precisely estimated (low posterior variance) in every specification. The net-present-value coefficient α is larger for higher-value home segments wherever the segment boundaries align, so higher-value households ascribe more weight to the economic benefit of a PV system: the low-value coefficient α[0 ,300) has eight mutually overlapping 95% credible intervals around 3 × 10 −4, the two-segment high-value coefficient overlapping intervals around 6.2 × 10 −4, and the three-segment [300 , 600) coefficient overlapping intervals around 8.5 × 10 −4, so no specification’s estimate is distin-guishable from the others at the 95% level; the single-segment models, which pool all home values into one economic segment, estimate a single coefficient near 4.1 × 10 −4. The only imprecisely identified coefficient is the top [600 , ∞)

bin, whose credible intervals are wide and rest on few very-high-value adopters, consistent with the expanding-window evidence in Appendix F. The neighborhood effect preserves the ordering γrural > γ suburban > γ urban in every one-mile-ring specification, and a ring standing alone recovers the same one-mile coefficients as when it is the inner ring of a two-ring model, the standalone and inner-ring γrural intervals overlapping (means 0.121 and 0.135 ). The neighborhood coefficient does scale with the ring width, which is mechanical: a half-mile ring aggregates far fewer installed systems, so the per-neighbor coefficient is larger and, in sparsely built rural areas, noisier (the half-mile rural coefficient carries wider credible intervals, for example (0 .13 , 0.40) in Model (8) against (0 .11 , 0.16) for the one-mile ring in Model (4)). This is why the half-mile specifications that match Model (4)H on out-of-sample error, such as Model (10), do so with a less stable and less interpretable near-neighbor coefficient and a geographic ordering that is no longer monotone. The one-mile ring therefore delivers the same forecast accuracy to within the noise while giving a well-identified, monotone neighborhood structure, and we use the one-mile radial ring, with two economic segments (Model (4)H), as the parsimonious specification of our model. Appendix F: Estimation with Expanding Data Window 

We assess the consistency of the estimates through an expanding-window re-estimation. We re-estimate the selected model, Model (4)H, on the first T quarters of the sample at even windows from T = 26 (2010Q2) through T = 36 

(2012Q4), together with the full estimation window T = 33 (2012Q1) reported in the paper, and track the posterior mean and 95% credible interval of each structural parameter as the window grows. Figure EC.5 plots the trajectories grouped by parameter family, on axes wide enough to span the estimates at every window; Table EC.6 reports the underlying per-window estimates. The net-present-value coefficients α of the two economic segments cross between windows T = 30 and T = 32 

(Figure EC.5b). In the early windows ( T ≤ 30 , through 2011Q2) the coefficient for the high-value [300K , ∞) segment sits below that of the majority [0 , 300K) segment, estimated near 2 × 10 −4: few high-value households have adopted by then, so their net-present-value coefficient has greater estimation error, reflected in wider interval estimates. As the window extends through the high-adoption quarters of 2011–2012, more high-value adoptions enter the sample and α[300K ,∞) rises, crossing above α[0 ,300K) at T = 32 and settling near its full-sample value of 6.4 × 10 −4 at T =33 . The crossover marks improved identification of the high-value segment’s price response rather than a change in preferences: once enough high-value adoptions are observed, the ordering α[300K ,∞) > α [0 ,300K) reported for the full sample is recovered and holds for every window from T = 32 onward. The remaining parameters support the stability reported in Section 5 of the paper (Figure EC.5; Table EC.6). The standard deviation of household heterogeneity σξ varies only between 0.0504 and 0.0515 , the baseline utility ρ stays within a narrow band ( −7.19 to −6.76 ), and the coefficient of the majority segment α[0 ,300K) holds its order of mag-nitude, near 3 to 4 × 10 −4. The coefficients γ for neighborhood effects are a little more sensitive to the sample sizes: their estimates are larger in the short early windows, with wider interval estimates, and gradually level off as the larger sampling window absorbs more data, when falling net costs account for a growing share of adoption. Their economic interpretation is nevertheless invariant over time: the ordering γrural > γ suburban > γ urban is preserved and no coefficient changes sign. By the full estimation window T = 33 , the heterogeneity scale, the baseline utility, and the majority-segment coefficient have settled and the high-value coefficient is identified, the stability a policymaker needs before relying on the estimates for counterfactual analysis. Benchmark (a): Hazard model (individual level) 

This is the reduced-form hazard model applied in Bollinger et al. (2022), estimated on our data. The discrete hazard benchmark model (15) in Section 5.1 is estimated by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), treating PV adoption as 

{0, 1} dummy variables over time in a discrete-time hazard process. For each household-period observation in which household i has not yet adopted, define yit = 1 if the household adopts in period t, and yit = 0 otherwise. The model specifies the conditional adoption probability, or hazard rate, as 

G (λit ) = ξz + ηt + β1NPV it + β2Xi + β3Hit + β4NPV Hit ,

where G(·) is either the logit link log( λit /(1 − λit )) or the complementary log-log link log( − log(1 − λit )) , both standard in discrete-time hazard models (Kalbfleisch and Prentice 2002); ξz are zip-code fixed effects that capture mar-ket and geographic factors, ηt is the temporal effect, modeled flexibly (polynomial, B-spline, natural spline, or local regression), and Xi collects the household characteristics: the total footprint of the house, the ratio of tree cover to footprint, the sunlight received, the hillshade, and the solar irradiance. The variables Hit and NPV Hit denote, respec-tively, the cumulative number of peer installations and the cumulative economic value (NPV) of those installations within the one-mile radial ring. Appendix H: Details of Differentiated Rebate Policy Simulations 

Geographic differentiation. We evaluate rebate policies that differentiate the rebate rate across the geographic seg-ments through a complete factorial: the net cost faced by urban, suburban, and rural households is each scaled by a multiplier in {0.8, 1.0, 1.2}, for 33 = 27 policies; a lower multiplier corresponds to a higher rebate rate. Each policy is one forward simulation of the selected model, Model (4)H, under a common rebate schedule. The simulation seeds on the first 16 quarters of observed adoption and generates new adoption from quarter 17 onward, so the reported horizon, period 56, corresponds to 10 years of simulated adoption. For each policy we record the cumulative adoption and the program budget at period 56. Figure 7 in the main text plots cumulative adoption against program budget for the 27 policies, the three-square glyph at each point encoding the urban, suburban, and rural multiplier (left, center, and right square) by color; Table EC.9 summarizes the results as regressions across the 27 policies. Cumulative adoption rises almost linearly with the program budget, from $38.4 million and 7,837 adopters at the most conservative policy to $45.0 million and 8,480 adopters at the most generous: column (1) of Table EC.9 regresses adoption on budget across the 27 policies and gives a slope of 94.9 adopters per million dollars with R2 = 0 .97 .Column (2) regresses adoption on the three multipliers directly: lowering any segment’s net cost raises total adoption, with the suburban multiplier the strongest lever (75 additional adopters per 0.1 discount, against 54 for urban and 31 for rural). Column (3) asks the budget-constrained question by regressing each policy’s deviation from the fitted line of column (1) on the multipliers, and the urban multiplier separates the policies: a 0.1 urban discount adds about 14 adopters beyond what its budget predicts, whereas suburban and rural discounts move a policy below the line. Accordingly, all nine policies that discount the urban net cost (multiplier 0.8) lie above the fitted line, by +13 to +52 

adopters ( +32 on average), whereas the policies that hold the urban net cost at its observed level or increase it lie below the line on average ( −8 and −25 adopters, respectively). This is the pattern visible in Figure 7 of the main text: the glyphs above the fitted relationship have the left (urban) square in green. The worked examples in Section 6.4 of the paper come from the same simulations. Appendix I: End-Horizon Sensitivity Analysis for Policy Simulations 

The cost-effectiveness comparisons of the rebate switching policies in Section 6.1 are made at period 56, 23 quarters (nearly 6 years) beyond the estimation window. We test whether those comparisons are robust to the end horizon by reconstructing the frontier analyses at five horizons, periods 40, 44, 48, 52, and 56 at four-period steps, equal to 2 through 6 years beyond the last quarter used for estimation (quarter 33). Because a one-step policy RSP( τ ) that switches at τ > T does not switch before the end horizon T and hence coincides with the no-switch schedule, we evaluate at each horizon only the policies that switch within it, τ ≤ T . Figure EC.7 reconstructs the frontier at each horizon: cumulative installed capacity against program budget on the left, cumulative carbon savings against program budget on the right; Table EC.10 lists the carbon-maximizing policy and its metrics at each horizon. Both features of the period-56 frontier reappear across the horizons. Installed capacity rises with budget as a concave curve, so each later switch yields less capacity gain and the costliest policies occupy the flat upper arm. Carbon savings are non-monotone in budget: at every horizon the curve has an interior maximum, with the switches before and after the optimum both below it. At every horizon, the carbon-maximizing one-step policy is an interior switch, never the most expensive policy: RSP(32) at the 2-year horizon and RSP(36) from 3 years onward. The budget at the optimum is about $30 million at 2 years and about $33 million from 3 years on, while the carbon level grows from 174.2 at 2 years to 604.2 at 6 years. The key reason for the interior optimum is the forward-looking behavior of households. Because households know the rebate is reduced at τ , an earlier switch concentrates installations in the quarters before it; that capacity operates sooner and accrues carbon savings over more of the horizon. A later switch installs marginally more capacity by the horizon’s end, 38.6 MW for RSP(48) against 36.4 MW for RSP(36) at period 56, yet accumulates less carbon, 552.3 against 604.2, because its capacity arrives later. Cumulative carbon savings, computed as the integral of installed capacity over time, reward the intermediate switch that front-loads adoption. Each policy’s program budget plateaus once its rebate is switched off, so it changes little at horizons beyond the switch; the optimum’s budget rises across horizons only because the optimal switch time itself moves later. The counterfactual conclusion therefore holds independently of the period-56 horizon. Evaluated over any window from 2 to 6 years beyond the estimation window, an intermediate switch yields the most carbon savings per budget dollar, installed capacity exhibits diminishing returns to budget, and deferring the switch past the interior optimum raises spending while lowering cumulative carbon. The cost-effective switch advances from RSP(32) at 2 years to RSP(36) from 3 years onward, and it is interior at every horizon.
